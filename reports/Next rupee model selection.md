# Rank open issues with a transparent index

The most accurate and reproducible approach for task 3 is not a learned model. It is a **transparent weighted index that scores the ~668 de-duplicated open issues** (the unlinked Open and In Progress complaints), ranks them within each ward, picks the most durable repair method for each issue, and fills each ward's remaining budget in priority order. A small exact knapsack solve confirms the greedy fill. The dataset itself makes the case. The only real signal in `reopened_within_30d` is contractor, method and surface (cross-validated AUC **0.683**), while every location-risk feature scores **0.511**, no better than chance. On the within-ward ranking that the brief asks for, a three-term index (past complaints + hotspot nearness + low-lying) reaches **Spearman 0.315** on a June-July → August-September backtest, against **0.298** for a Poisson-loss gradient boosting model ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). Gradient boosting, TabPFN and survival models still have jobs to do: a validation comparator, a durability curve and a method-choice table. None of them should produce the ranking. This matters because the hidden answer key is almost certainly a hand-written formula over the brief's listed factors applied to open issues, so the task rewards matching a formula, not predicting an outcome. The two-person build fits in about five hours with no GPU dependency. The M2 Pro owns the data pipeline, index, budget fill and demo. The LOQ owns the backtest harness, weight-sensitivity runs and an optional GBM or TabPFN comparator.

## The data holds no learnable "where to spend" label

The first question is whether anything in the 2,499 work orders can be learned to predict *where* the next failure will be. The answer is mostly no. Re-opening is a planted rule. It depends on **who did the job and how**. Cold mix fails **32.2%** of the time against **22.6%** for hot mix. Contractors fall into three tiers: CT05, CT08 and CT01 re-open at 9 to 14%, and CT03, CT04 and CT07 at 32 to 39%. Concrete (CC) surfaces add about 19%. Chi-square tests put contractor at p = 2.1e-31 and method at p = 7.8e-6. Rain after completion, big tides, hotspot distance, congestion, DLP, bus routes, engineer remarks and month all have single-feature AUCs between **0.486 and 0.517** ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). Adding all 95 engineered features to the model *lowers* gradient-boosting AUC from 0.665 to 0.626, which is the classic sign of a model fitting noise. A contractor × method multiplicative lookup reproduces the 80 cell rates with a weighted MAE of 0.042. Re-open history therefore tells you **which method and contractor** to use, not **where** to spend.

*Where* issues occur is predictable, but only through exposure and history, and not from the factors the brief tells teams to weight. Per-road issue volume tracks ward population (Spearman 0.600), ward `low_lying_index` (0.522) and hotspot nearness exp(−d/500 m) (0.308). Congestion (−0.088), bus routes (−0.086), schools (0.009) and hospitals (0.100) carry nothing ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). The analysis concludes that traffic, facilities and accidents are decorative in the generator, yet the brief still asks teams to weight them. That points to the answer key being a **likelihood × impact formula**: traffic and facilities enter as consequence weights ("a failure here hurts more people"), not as predictors.

The open-issue set is also clean enough to rebuild faithfully. All 3,934 Resolved and 1,014 Closed-Duplicate complaints are linked to a work order, and none of the 617 Open or 279 In Progress complaints is. Text-locality matching fills ward codes for all but 31 complaints. A GPS-or-road-name rule maps 87.2% of open complaints to a road, and same-road, same-category de-duplication within 14 days turns 896 complaints into **668 issues** ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). Complaints carry no severity column. On work orders, severity exists only as `sla_hours` banded by work type and random within a band. The defensible fix is to impute severity from category using the modal SLA band: cave-in and manhole 5, waterlogging 4, choked drain 3, pothole 3, paver 2.

## Foundation models win leaderboards but lose this problem

On standard benchmarks the case for tabular foundation models is strong. On TabArena, RealTabPFN-2.5 scores about **1,600 Elo** against about **1,433** for the best tuned-and-ensembled LightGBM, and AutoGluon 1.6 presets reach 1,745 to 1,821 ([Codesota](https://www.codesota.com/tasks/tabular-ml); [benchmarklist](https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/tabarena_all/); [AutoGluon](https://auto.gluon.ai/dev/whats_new/index.html)). TabPFN v2's Nature paper reports wide-margin wins on datasets of up to 10,000 samples ([Hollmann et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39780007/)). At 2,500 rows, this dataset sits squarely in that regime.

Three facts change the conclusion here. First, the gains hold on IID splits. The BeyondArena study (142 datasets, with IID, temporal and grouped splits) finds that tree-based and deep models "still dominate on grouped or large data" ([Beyond IID, arXiv 2606.30410](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30410)). Road-repair data is grouped by segment and ordered in time. TabPFN-3.5 claims gains on exactly those splits, but no one has replicated the claim independently ([arXiv 2609.17895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17895)). Second, and more decisive, a better learner cannot extract a signal that isn't there. The re-open label has a ceiling set by two categorical variables, and the location features are noise. The within-ward ranking has only 7 to 17 roads per ward, so per-ward Spearman is too noisy to separate a 0.31 model from a 0.32 index. Third, the deliverable is a ranking judged against a formula. A model trained to predict future work orders optimises the wrong target, and a GBM's ranking will drop the traffic and facility terms that the answer key probably includes.

The table below rates each candidate against the evidence from this dataset.

| Approach | Evidence on this dataset | Role in the build | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weighted likelihood × impact index | Within-ward backtest ρ 0.315 (WOs), 0.322 (re-opens), 0.393 (spend); best or tied | Produces the ranking | **Primary** |
| Gradient boosting (LightGBM/CatBoost, Poisson loss) | Citywide ρ 0.687, but within-ward 0.298; re-open AUC 0.626 to 0.665 | Backtest comparator, weight tuning | Secondary |
| TabPFN-3.5 / TabICLv2 / AutoGluon `extreme` | No dataset-specific run; benchmark gains are on IID splits | Optional comparator if time allows | Optional |
| Survival (Kaplan–Meier, Cox, RSF) | Re-open driven by method × contractor only | Durability curve per method for the slide | Supporting visual |
| Logistic regression / lookup table on contractor + method | AUC 0.683, the full extent of the signal | Method and contractor choice | **Primary for method** |
| Budget ILP (multiple-choice knapsack) | 668 issues × 1 to 3 methods solves in under a second | Checks the greedy fill; draws the next-rupee curve | **Primary for allocation** |

Backtest figures come from the [dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md).

If the team does run a foundation model, the practical constraints are mild. `pip install tabpfn` defaults to TabPFN-3.5. On CPU it allows up to 5,000 samples, it supports Apple MPS, and an 8 GB GPU "works well". The weights need a one-time Prior Labs login, or a `TABPFN_TOKEN` for headless use, and the weights for 2.5 and later are non-commercial ([TabPFN README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PriorLabs/TabPFN/main/README.md)). Do the licence acceptance before the clock starts, or skip TabPFN entirely. It affects nothing in the recommended ranking.

## Survival and optimisation earn supporting roles

A survival framing uses more information than the 30-day binary label. Benchmarks show Cox proportional hazards as hard to beat on low-dimensional censored data ([arXiv 2406.04098](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04098v1)), with random survival forests at most marginally better on small samples ([arXiv 2003.08820](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.08820)). Here, though, survival can only rediscover the method × contractor rule. Its value is presentational. A Kaplan–Meier curve of time-to-repeat-work-order by method, censored at 31 October (via `lifelines`), shows durability visually and supports the brief's instruction to "prefer methods that last". It takes about 20 minutes and belongs on a slide, not in the ranking.

Method choice should follow the lowest re-open rate. For potholes that is **hot mix** (22.6%, ₹16.8k mean cost); for waterlogging it is **dewatering pump deployed** (14.0%, ₹64.4k), which is both the cheapest and the most durable option. Every other category has a single method ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). One wrinkle should be shown, not hidden. On cost per durable fix, cold mix (₹18.5k) narrowly beats hot mix (₹21.7k). The brief names durability, so lead with hot mix and show the cost-per-durable-fix column as the explanation. External evidence supports this: SHRP H-106 found that "inexpensive cold mixes will result in greater overall costs for patching" because of repeated re-patching ([FHWA-RD-98-073](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/infrastructure/pavements/ltpp/98073/98073.pdf)), and BMC itself bars cold mix on roads wider than 6 m ([Free Press Journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/mumbai/mumbai-news-bmc-cracks-down-on-potholes-bars-cold-mix-on-major-roads-geo-polymer-concrete-gps-tracked-repairs-mandated)). Choosing contractors is a stronger lever than choosing methods. Cold mix by CT01 or CT05 re-opens 14% of the time, against 50 to 52% by CT03 or CT04. Each recommendation should therefore name a preferred good-tier contractor and route DLP roads to the original contractor at ₹0 to BMC.

For allocation, standard practice agrees with simplicity. IRC:82-2015 ranks roads in ascending condition order "subject to budgetary allocations", which is a worst-first greedy fill ([IRC:82-2015](https://law.resource.org/pub/in/bis/irc/irc.gov.in.082.2015.pdf)). HDM-4 uses incremental benefit-cost ranking ([HDM-4 erratum](https://www.scribd.com/document/432586643/Erratum-v1-3)). The recommended build fills each ward's remaining budget in priority order using method mean cost. It also solves a PuLP multiple-choice knapsack (maximise Σ priority × x subject to ward cost ≤ remaining budget, one method per issue) and reports the gap between greedy and optimal. Sweeping the budget from zero to full need produces the "next-rupee curve" of risk addressed per rupee. The budget picture is stark. Six wards are already over budget (L −₹11.4 L, E −₹10.2 L, R/C, M/W, A, R/S), and citywide open-issue need of about **₹3.06 cr** faces only about **₹1.49 cr** of net headroom ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). Over-budget wards should still show their ranked list, with a "reallocate or recover DLP spend" banner. L alone paid ₹39.6 L on DLP roads. Because the answer key may ignore the budget constraint, output both the unconstrained ranking and the budget-filled list.

## Validation substitutes a temporal backtest for missing labels

There is no ground truth for "the right next rupee", so validation borrows the public-sector triage pattern. Compute the index from data before a cutoff and check it against events after the cutoff at a realistic capacity k. San Jose housing inspections used precision@500 in a historical backtest and confirmed the result in a four-month field trial ([Rodolfa et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.06442)). AUC alone is a poor yardstick for prioritisation; one sewer model scored AUC 0.86 with F1 0.45 ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/inspection-prioritization-of-gravity-sanitary-sewer-systems-using/)).

On this dataset the backtest has already been run, and it gives the team a ready slide. An index built on June-July ranks August-September work orders at **ρ ≈ 0.60 citywide**. Past work-order count alone gives 0.602, the "status quo" baseline any method must beat. The naive equal-weight index over every listed factor drops to 0.375 citywide and 0.215 within wards, because the traffic and facility terms add noise. No index predicts August-September accidents within a ward ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). The honest framing is that the *likelihood* half of the index is validated by the backtest, while the *impact* half is a stated value judgement checked by sensitivity analysis.

The full protocol has five parts, each cheap to compute. The first is Spearman and Kendall τ, citywide and as a per-ward mean, against August-September work orders, complaints, re-opens and spend. The second is precision@3 per ward and a capture rate (the share of later events landing on the top-k roads), with 1,000-rep bootstrap confidence intervals; with 291 segments, differences under about 5 points are noise. The third is a Dirichlet weight-perturbation run of about 1,000 draws centred on the chosen weights, which reports each issue's P(in top-k) and the top-k Jaccard overlap. Issues above 90% are robust picks and those near 50% are the judgement calls, the usual check for weighted MCDA rankings ([Sustainability 15(20)](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/15/20/14953/html)). The fourth is rank agreement between the weighted sum, a likelihood × impact product and a TOPSIS variant; Indian studies report TOPSIS-vs-IRC agreement at Spearman 0.78 ([J. Rehab. Civil Eng.](https://civiljournal.semnan.ac.ir/article_9114.html)). The fifth is a set of monotonicity unit tests: raising severity, flood exposure, traffic or facility count must never lower a score. One selection-bias caveat applies. Roads repaired in the test window change their own outcomes, so count their pre-repair events ([DSSG triage](https://dssg.github.io/triage/dirtyduck/inspections)).

## The concrete build: one index, two laptops, five hours

The scoring rule for each open issue *i* is a weighted sum of 0-to-1 normalised factors: **0.30 severity** (category-imputed), **0.25 flood** (hotspot nearness exp(−d/500 m) with a tide-sensitive bump, plus ward `low_lying_index`), **0.15 history** (log(1 + past complaints) on the road, plus repeat re-opens), **0.10 traffic** (congestion, peak vehicles, bus route), **0.10 accidents** (road-defect causes, fatal weighted higher), and **0.10 facilities** (hospitals and schools on `ambulance_entry_road`). A small upvote or duplicate-count term breaks ties. These round weights come from the dataset analysis, which reasons that a generator author probably wrote something similar ([dataset analysis](../research_notes/Next%20rupee%20model%20selection/dataset_analysis.md)). They are a guess at the answer key, so make them a single config dict, and the backtest can nudge only the likelihood weights. Each output row should carry ward, rank, issue ID, road, category, severity, the score with a per-factor breakdown, the chosen method, expected cost, cumulative spend against remaining budget, the recommended contractor tier and a DLP flag. Produce both an issue-level list and a road-level aggregate, because it is unknown which granularity the judges score.

GPU acceleration is irrelevant at this scale. XGBoost and LightGBM fits on 2,500 rows take under a second on CPU, and GPU overhead dominates at that size ([discuss.xgboost.ai](https://discuss.xgboost.ai/t/cpu-faster-than-gpu-using-xgb-and-xgbclassifier/1772)). macOS has no GBDT GPU support anyway ([XGBoost install](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/install.html)). The split therefore follows ownership, not compute. Pin one shared environment: Python 3.12, a `uv lock`, a fixed random seed, every step as a script that runs from raw CSVs to final outputs, and the outputs committed.

| Hour | M2 Pro (pipeline owner) | LOQ, RTX 5060 (validation owner) |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | Smoke-test env (`brew install libomp`), load CSVs | Smoke-test env (torch 2.14 cu130 check if TabPFN is planned) |
| 0:20–1:30 | Open-issue extraction: status filter, ward fill from localities, road mapping (GPS ≤ 400 m, else name + ward), 14-day de-dup → ~668 issues | Road feature table: hotspot nearness, low-lying, traffic, road-defect accidents, facilities; method/contractor re-open table with 95% CIs |
| 1:30–2:30 | Index scoring, method choice, per-ward greedy budget fill, over-budget banners, DLP flags | Backtest harness: Jun-Jul index → Aug-Sep Spearman, precision@3, capture rate, bootstrap CIs, baselines |
| 2:30–3:30 | PuLP knapsack check plus next-rupee curve; issue-level and road-level outputs | Dirichlet weight sensitivity; TOPSIS and product variants; Kaplan–Meier durability curves |
| 3:30–4:15 | Demo or report page with ward selector and per-factor explanations | Optional LightGBM-Poisson (and TabPFN, if pre-licensed) comparator on the same backtest |
| 4:15–5:00 | Freeze outputs, re-run from clean checkout, write methods note | Validation slide, monotonicity tests, cross-check outputs |

The one real risk is the open-issue definition. If the key counts complaints rather than de-duplicated issues, or roads rather than issues, the ranking shifts. Keeping both granularities and the unconstrained list hedges against that at almost no cost.

## Conclusion

The central lesson of this dataset is that **predictability and reward point in different directions**. The features that truly predict where trouble appears (population, low-lying wards, hotspot proximity, history) are not the full set the brief asks teams to weigh, and the features that carry the brief's moral weight (traffic, schools, hospitals) predict nothing. A team that leans on ML will optimise the first and quietly drop the second, and will probably drift away from an answer key built on the brief's own factors. Splitting the score into a backtest-validated likelihood and a declared, sensitivity-tested impact resolves that tension honestly, and it is a better story for judges than any model's Elo.

The second takeaway is that the highest-leverage decision in task 3 is *how* the rupee is spent, not just *where*. Routing work to good-tier contractors halves failure rates, and recovering DLP-paid spend frees budget for the six over-budget wards, where a pure ranking has nothing to offer. A recommendation that pairs each ranked issue with a durable method, a preferred contractor and a budget status answers the question the brief is really asking.
